## Description
EPOP (Elongin BC and Polycomb repressive complex 2-associated protein, also known as C17orf96 or PRR28) is a 379 amino acid intracellular protein localized to the nucleoplasm, cytosol, and cytoplasmic bodies (UniProt A6NHQ4) with medium-confidence partially-disordered architecture (AlphaFold pLDDT 58.5), suggesting a hybrid of an ordered C-terminal domain and extensive N-terminal intrinsically disordered regions (IDR) critical for dynamic chromatin interactions. The protein defines the EPOP-like family (IPR027971/PTHR23187, PF15223, residues 305-371) and the Elongin BC-PRC2 Interaction & Viral Restriction family (IPR052119/PTHR23187, residues 298-376). The C-terminal domain (~75 aa) mediates binding to the Elongin BC complex, while the N-terminal region contains proline-rich extensions (PRICHEXTENSN PR01217, residues 150-162/170-186/193-205) likely involved in protein-protein interactions within the Polycomb machinery. Molecular functions include chromatin binding, regulation of transcription by RNA polymerase II, and neuron fate commitment, placing EPOP within the epigenetic silencing pathway.\n\nSTRING interaction analysis reveals an exceptionally tight network within the PRC2.1 holocomplex: near-perfect interactions with PHF19 (0.997, Polycomb-like 3), MTF2 (0.963, Metal-response element-binding transcription factor 2), EZH2 (0.947, Enhancer of zeste homolog 2, catalytic subunit), SUZ12 (0.945, Suppressor of zeste 12), and PHF1 (0.940, Polycomb-like 1). High confidence links include AEBP2 (0.937), JARID2 (0.914), EZH1 (0.914), EED (0.875), and RBBP7 (0.859). This profile confirms EPOP as a specific accessory subunit of the PRC2.1 variant (containing PCL proteins PHF1/MTF2/PHF19), distinct from PRC2.2. EPOP acts as a physical bridge, recruiting the Elongin BC complex (ELOB/ELOC) to PRC2.1, thereby modulating its enzymatic activity and chromatin targeting.\n\nHPA expression reveals a tissue-enhanced pattern with top expression in brain (13.5 nTPM) and testis (11.1 nTPM). This distribution aligns with roles in neurodevelopment (Hox gene regulation) and germline epigenetic programming. Subcellular localization to the nucleoplasm and cytoplasmic bodies suggests shuttling between chromatin and storage/assembly sites. Low cancer specificity indicates a physiological rather than oncogenic driver role, though PRC2 dysregulation is common in cancer.\n\nClinVar documents 7 pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants, exclusively large copy number gains and losses affecting chromosome 17q (17q12-21.2 duplications, 17q12-22 deletions, 17p11.2-q21.2 duplications). The variant burden indicates dosage sensitivity. While no specific Mendelian disease is annotated, EPOP haploinsufficiency or overexpression likely causes developmental delay, axial skeletal defects, or neurological abnormalities due to disrupted Hox gene repression. 17q CNVs are associated with 17q12 deletion syndrome (renal cysts, diabetes, neurodevelopmental issues) and 17q21.31 microdeletion syndrome (Koolen-de Vries syndrome features).\n\nConservation is strong across mammals: human (A6NHQ4, 379 aa), mouse (Q7TNS8, 369 aa, 73.8% identity), indicating evolutionary pressure to maintain the Elongin-binding interface.\n\nMechanistic hypothesis: EPOP functions as a regulatory switch for PRC2.1 activity. By binding both Elongin BC and the PRC2.1 core (via PHF19/MTF2), EPOP stabilizes the complex on chromatin and fine-tunes H3K27me3 deposition at developmental genes (e.g., Hox clusters). Recent literature (PMID:41519789) suggests EPOP restricts PRC2.1 targeting by modulating enzyme dimerization, preventing aberrant silencing. In the brain, this ensures precise spatiotemporal gene expression during axial patterning and neuron differentiation. Loss of EPOP disrupts this balance, leading to ectopic gene activation or silencing, manifesting as developmental delay or skeletal malformations. The disordered N-terminus may allow conformational flexibility to accommodate different PRC2.1 compositions. EPOP represents a potential biomarker for 17q-related syndromes and a target for modulating Polycomb activity in regenerative medicine.
