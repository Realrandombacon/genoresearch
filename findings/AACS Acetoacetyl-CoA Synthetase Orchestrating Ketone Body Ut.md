# AACS: Acetoacetyl-CoA Synthetase Orchestrating Ketone Body Utilization, Cholesterol Biosynthesis, and Neurodegeneration via AMP-Dependent Ligase Activity Connecting Ketolysis to Lipogenesis in Metabolic Syndrome and Parkinson's Disease

## Description
AACS (Acetoacetyl-CoA Synthetase) is a 672 amino acid AMP-dependent ligase that catalyzes the activation of acetoacetate to acetoacetyl-CoA using ATP and CoA, serving as a critical metabolic node connecting ketone body utilization to cholesterol biosynthesis and fatty acid metabolism through conversion of circulating ketones into lipogenic precursors. InterPro analysis identifies characteristic AMP-binding enzyme architecture including central AMP-dependent synthetase/ligase domain (IPR000873, PF00501, residues 110-488) performing acetoacetate-CoA ligase activity (GO:0030729) and lipid metabolic process (GO:0006629), acetoacetyl-CoA synthase family signature (IPR005914, residues 16-670) defining the AACS enzyme family, conserved AMP-binding site (IPR020845, PS00455, residues 287-298) with catalytic residues for ATP-dependent adenylation, acetyl-CoA synthetase N-terminal domain (IPR032387, PF16177, residues 47-104) for substrate recognition, ANL N-terminal domain superfamily (IPR042099, G3DSA:3.40.50.12780, residues 25-541) forming the adenylation catalytic core, and C-terminal structural domain (G3DSA:3.30.300.30, residues 543-664) completing the bilobal enzyme structure. AlphaFold structure (AF-Q86V21-F1) predicts very high confidence (pLDDT 91.8) across the 672 residue sequence, indicating an exceptionally well-folded metabolic enzyme with stable catalytic domains suitable for ATP-dependent substrate activation. HPA data classifies AACS as an enzymatic metabolic protein involved in fatty acid and lipid metabolism with ligase molecular function, vesicular subcellular localization reflecting lipid droplet or peroxisomal association, low tissue specificity (detected in all tissues), and cell type-enhanced distribution consistent with ubiquitous metabolic housekeeping function. STRING interactions reveal tight functional coupling to ketone body metabolism and cholesterol biosynthesis pathways: HMGCL (3-Hydroxymethylglutaryl-CoA lyase, 0.962) for ketolysis; ACAT2 (Acetyl-CoA acetyltransferase 2, 0.958) for acetoacetyl-CoA cleavage to acetyl-CoA; OXCT1 (Succinyl-CoA:3-ketoacid CoA transferase 1, 0.951) as the rate-limiting ketolysis enzyme; HMGCS2 (HMG-CoA synthase 2, 0.944) for mitochondrial ketogenesis; HMGCLL1 (HMG-CoA lyase-like 1, 0.943) as a paralog; OXCT2 (0.942) as an extrahepatic ketolysis isoform; HMGCS1 (0.917) for cytosolic cholesterol synthesis; ACAT1 (0.498) for mitochondrial acetoacetyl-CoA metabolism; EHHADH (0.484) for peroxisomal fatty acid oxidation; and BDH2 (0.482) for beta-hydroxybutyrate metabolism. ClinVar lists 25 pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants including multiple copy number gains on 12q24.21-24.33, 12q24.22-24.33, 12q24.31-24.33 (spanning 12-17 Mb regions), copy number loss on 12q24.31-24.32, and splice site variants (c.1678+6T>G, c.996+223T>G), though no specific monogenic syndrome is defined, suggesting potential roles in inborn errors of ketone metabolism or lipid disorder. Literature (24,669 papers, though many address unrelated 'AACS' acronyms; gene-specific studies establish metabolic roles) identifies AACS as a biomarker in Parkinson's disease linked to lipid metabolic disorders, associates AACS haplotypes with insulin resistance in metabolic syndrome through branched chain amino acid metabolism regulation, reveals AACS upregulation in hepatocellular carcinoma promoting lipid accumulation and tumor progression, demonstrates AACS contribution to de novo lipogenesis from ketone bodies in adipose tissue and liver, and establishes AACS as a therapeutic target connecting ketone metabolism to cholesterol biosynthesis and neurodegeneration. As a well-characterized metabolic enzyme with emerging disease associations, AACS represents a critical node connecting systemic ketone body utilization to intracellular lipid synthesis, cholesterol homeostasis, and neurodegenerative pathology through ATP-dependent acetoacetate activation and metabolic channeling to acetyl-CoA pools.
