# Mitochondrial Translation Initiation Factor 2 with Emerging Roles in Mitoribosome Assembly and Cancer Progression

## Description
MTIF2 (727 aa, UniProt P46199) is a mitochondrial translational initiation factor that promotes the binding of initiator tRNA to the small mitoribosomal subunit in a GTP-dependent manner, essential for oxidative phosphorylation complex synthesis. The protein contains a bacterial-like IF-2 architecture (IPR000178) with a translational GTP-binding domain (IPR000795/PF00009, aa178-348) featuring the canonical P-loop motif for nucleotide hydrolysis, and distinct C-terminal domains (CD03692, aa624-711) critical for ribosomal docking. Recent 2024-2026 studies reveal dual roles: (1) as a mitoribosomal small subunit assembly factor cooperating with pseudouridine synthase PUS1, and (2) as an oncogenic driver in hepatocellular carcinoma where it modulates ROS pathways to impair 5-fluorouracil-mediated immunogenic cell death. STRING interactions confirm tight functional coupling with the mitoribosomal machinery, including MRPS16 (0.992), MRPS6 (0.992), MRPS5 (0.991), MRPL9 (0.989), and the partner initiation factor MTIF3 (0.987). HPA data shows ubiquitous expression with specific mitochondrial localization, consistent with its housekeeping role in energy metabolism. AlphaFold prediction (pLDDT 84.2) reveals a confident multi-domain structure with a rigid G-domain and flexible interdomain linkers likely facilitating conformational changes during initiation. Clinical relevance is supported by 14 pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants in ClinVar, including frameshifts (c.899_909del p.Val300fs) and large CNVs at 2p16.1, suggesting dosage sensitivity. Conservation is high across eukaryotes due to the prokaryotic origin of mitochondria. Mechanistically, MTIF2 acts as a molecular switch: GTP binding stabilizes the fMet-tRNA-mitoribosome complex, while hydrolysis triggers subunit joining; dysregulation leads to defective respiratory chain assembly (bioenergetic failure) or, in cancer contexts, enhanced ROS scavenging and survival.
