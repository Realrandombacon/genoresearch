## Description
SUGP1 (UniProt Q8IWZ8), also known as SF4, encodes a 645 aa SURP module and G-patch domain-containing protein functioning as a critical spliceosomal regulator that controls 3′ splice site recognition through direct interaction with SF3B1, the most frequently mutated splicing factor in cancer. InterPro analysis reveals bipartite domain architecture: dual SURP (Suppressor-of-White-APricot) modules (G3DSA:1.10.10.790/IPR000061/IPR035967/PF1805/PS50128/SM00648, residues 162-248/249-324/184-248/258-322/191-233/266-309) mediating RNA binding (GO:0003723) and RNA processing (GO:0006396); C-terminal G-patch domain (IPR000467/PF01585/PS50174/SM00443, residues 560-609/563-606/562-609) providing glycine-rich nucleic acid binding (GO:0003676) and protein-protein interaction capacity; and SUGP1/2 family membership (IPR040169/PTHR23340, residues 24-645) defining arginine/serine-rich splicing factor function. AlphaFold predicts medium confidence structure (pLDDT 64.4) indicating partially folded protein with ordered SURP domains connected by flexible linkers, consistent with intrinsically disordered regions enabling dynamic spliceosome assembly. The perfect DHX15 score and strong U2AF2/RBM links confirm SUGP1 as integral U2 snRNP component regulating early spliceosome assembly. HPA classifies SUGP1 as essential protein/predicted intracellular protein with ubiquitous expression (detected in all tissues, low tissue specificity), localized to nucleoplasm consistent with splicing function, functioning in mRNA processing/mRNA splicing, with cell type enhanced specificity. ClinVar lists 11 pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants including intronic/regulatory SNVs (c.207-58G>A, c.663-73A>G, c.1734C>T p.Gly578=, c.1282C>A p.Pro428Thr) and large copy number gains (19p13.12-12x3, 19p13.3-q13.43x3, 19p13.2-q13.31x3, 19p13.12-q11x3); while no single-gene disorder name is assigned, CNVs affecting 19p13 region cause recognizable syndromes where SUGP1 dosage may contribute to neurodevelopmental and metabolic phenotypes. Functional hypothesis: SUGP1 operates through integrated RNA recognition-protein interaction mechanisms: (1) SF3B1 interface - Recent 2023-2025 structural studies reveal SUGP1 binds SF3B1 HEAT domain near cancer mutation hotspots (K700E, H662Q, R625H); SUGP1 loss phenocopies SF3B1 mutations causing aberrant 3′ splice site usage; SUGP1 acts as molecular brake preventing premature branch point recognition; (2) Cancer mechanism - SF3B1 mutations disrupt SUGP1 binding, releasing brake on cryptic 3′ splice sites; SUGP1 loss drives missplicing in SF3B1-mutant cancers (myelodysplastic syndromes, uveal melanoma, chronic lymphocytic leukemia); (3) Dual domain function - N-terminal SURP modules recognize specific RNA sequences at branch point/3′ splice junction; C-terminal G-patch recruits helicases (DHX15) and other RBPs for spliceosome remodeling; (4) piRNA biogenesis - 2025 literature shows SUGP1 associates with Y-box protein regulating piRNA biogenesis in Bombyx mori, suggesting conserved role in small RNA pathways; (5) Metabolic regulation - 2024 study links SUGP1 variants to liver fat accumulation in normal-weight individuals, suggesting splicing-dependent metabolic control; (6) Conservation - Mouse Q8CH02 (643 aa), Rat Q68FU8 (644 aa), Arabidopsis homolog (443 aa) indicate conserved splicing function across eukaryotes. Dark aspects requiring investigation include: precise RNA sequence specificity of SURP modules, structural mechanism of SF3B1-SUGP1 interface disruption by cancer mutations, tissue-specific thresholds for SUGP1 dosage (why liver most vulnerable to metabolic phenotypes), therapeutic potential of restoring SUGP1-SF3B1 interaction in SF3B1-mutant cancers, whether SUGP1 has SF3B1-independent functions in piRNA biogenesis in mammals, contribution to neurodevelopmental disorders via 19p13 CNVs, crosstalk between SUGP1 and other RBM proteins (RBM10/17/25/39/5), regulation by post-translational modifications (phosphorylation, methylation), impact of intronic variants on splicing efficiency, potential synthetic lethality with SF3B1 inhibitors in cancer, and whether heterozygous carriers have subtle splicing defects or cancer predisposition.
