# MICOS10: Core MICOS Complex Subunit Driving Mitochondrial Cristae Junction Formation and Linked to Hepatocerebral mtDNA Depletion Syndrome

## Description
MICOS10 (Mitochondrial Contact Site and Cristae Organizing System subunit 10, also known as MIC10) is a critical but under-studied structural component of the mitochondrial inner membrane with only 4 publications, recently identified (2024) as the causative gene for a novel form of hepatocerebral mitochondrial DNA depletion syndrome. This small 78-amino acid protein (UniProt Q5TGZ0) contains a conserved Domain of Unknown Function 543 (DUF543/PF04418, positions 2-62) which defines the MICOS complex subunit Mic10 family (IPR007512). Despite its small size, AlphaFold predicts a confident structure (pLDDT 73.2), suggesting a stable transmembrane fold essential for membrane remodeling.\n\nSTRING interactions reveal MICOS10 operates at the heart of the MICOS complex machinery with exceptionally high confidence scores: CHCHD3/MIC19 (0.999), IMMT/MIC60 (0.999), APOOL/MIC27 (0.993), APOO/MIC26 (0.989), MICOS13/MIC13 (0.984), SAMM50 (0.966), MTX1 (0.943), DNAJC11 (0.938), MTX2 (0.936), and CHCHD6 (0.919). This interaction network confirms MICOS10's role as a core scaffold bridging the inner and outer mitochondrial membranes to maintain cristae junction integrity.\n\nHPA shows ubiquitous expression detected in all tissues with specific localization to mitochondria, consistent with its fundamental role in organelle architecture. ClinVar contains 16 pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants, predominantly large copy number losses and gains on chromosome 1p36, disrupting the MICOS10 locus. The recent discovery of biallelic variants causing hepatocerebral mtDNA depletion syndrome highlights its non-redundant function in mitochondrial genome maintenance.\n\nHYPOTHESIS: MICOS10 is the primary membrane-remodeling engine of the MICOS complex, utilizing its small hydrophobic DUF543 domain to oligomerize and induce high-curvature membranes at cristae junctions. Unlike larger MICOS subunits that serve as scaffolds (MIC60) or tethers (MIC19), MICOS10 likely acts as the structural wedge that physically bends the inner membrane. Its interaction with SAMM50 links cristae formation to the sorting and assembly machinery of the outer membrane. The severe phenotype of MICOS10 deficiency (hepatocerebral syndrome) suggests that loss of cristae junctions leads to collapse of the proton gradient and failure of oxidative phosphorylation, particularly affecting high-energy tissues like liver and brain. Pathogenic CNVs disrupt this delicate architecture, causing secondary mtDNA instability due to impaired nucleoid organization at cristae tips.
