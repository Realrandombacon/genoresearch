## Description
ACOT11 (Acyl-Coenzyme A Thioesterase 11, also known as BFIT or STARD14) is a 607-amino acid cytosolic metabolic enzyme (UniProt Q8WXI4) representing an emerging dark gene with 39 publications despite its critical newly-discovered roles regulating macrophage IL-1β maturation via IFNGR2 palmitoylation, fatty acid metabolism in gastric cancer, lung squamous cell carcinoma biomarker function, lung adenocarcinoma progression, autophagy enhancement, and 1p34-32 copy number variation disorders. Structurally, ACOT11 contains unique dual-domain architecture combining catalytic thioesterase modules with lipid-binding capacity: two tandem hotdog acyl-CoA thioesterase domains (IPR033120/cd03442, residues 43-155/43-157 and 216-329/213-336) forming the catalytic core with characteristic alpha-beta hotdog fold for acyl-CoA substrate binding and hydrolysis, thioesterase superfamily domains (IPR006683/PF03061, residues 67-133 and 234-304) providing enzymatic active sites, C-terminal START domain (IPR002913/PF01852/cd08913, residues 384-590/385-588/344-583) mediating lipid/sterol binding for substrate presentation or product sequestration, hotdog thioesterase superfamily (G3DSA:3.10.129.10, residues 39-192 and 193-337) defining structural fold, START-like domain superfamily (IPR023393/G3DSA:3.30.530.20, residues 338-560) providing lipid-binding scaffold, and cytosolic acyl-CoA thioester hydrolase family (IPR040170, residues 44-370) defining functional class. Recent literature establishes emerging specialized roles: porcine GWAS identifies ACOT11 as regulator for macrophage IL-1β maturation via IFNGR2 palmitoylation demonstrating direct post-translational regulatory mechanism controlling inflammation (12 citations, 2025); salidroside suppresses gastric cancer progression via miR-1343-3p-mediated repression of ACOT11 and disruption of fatty acid metabolism establishing tumor metabolic dependency (2 citations, 2025); ACOT11 as novel biomarker and therapeutic target in lung squamous cell carcinoma (0 citations, 2025); ACOT11 promotes cell proliferation, migration and invasion in lung adenocarcinoma establishing oncogenic function (26 citations, 2020); human START domain of ACOT11 bound to myristic acid revealing lipid-binding mechanism (0 citations, 2020). The mechanistic hypothesis: ACOT11 functions as a dual-function metabolic enzyme that hydrolyzes fatty acyl-CoA esters while simultaneously regulating protein palmitoylation and lipid signaling. The N-terminal tandem hotdog domains (residues 43-336) contain catalytic residues (conserved aspartate/glutamate) coordinating water molecule for nucleophilic attack on thioester bond, releasing free fatty acid and CoA-SH. The C-terminal START domain (residues 384-590) binds hydrophobic ligands (myristic acid demonstrated, likely other saturated/unsaturated fatty acids) presenting substrates to catalytic sites or sequestering products. In macrophages, ACOT11 regulates IFNGR2 palmitoylation status—by controlling local palmitoyl-CoA pools, ACOT11 modulates DHHC palmitoyltransferase activity on IFNGR2, affecting receptor trafficking and IL-1β maturation. In gastric cancer, miR-1343-3p repression of ACOT11 disrupts fatty acid metabolism reducing tumor growth. In lung cancer, ACOT11 overexpression enhances proliferation/migration/invasion potentially through altered lipid signaling (lysophosphatidic acid, prostaglandins) or energy metabolism. The RUBCNL interaction suggests ACOT11 couples fatty acid metabolism to autophagosome maturation, possibly by generating free fatty acids that serve as autophagy signals or membrane components. The SLCO1A2 connection indicates ACOT11 may coordinate with organic anion transporters for metabolite exchange. The intestinal enrichment (32.2 nTPM) suggests ACOT11 regulates dietary fatty acid processing. The immune cell enhancement indicates inflammatory regulation role. The 1p34-32 CNV dosage changes alter ACOT11 expression: deletion reduces thioesterase activity causing fatty acyl-CoA accumulation (lipotoxicity, impaired palmitoylation); duplication increases fatty acid hydrolysis potentially depleting signaling lipids. The absence of formal disease associations despite 17 pathogenic variants suggests ACOT11 dysfunction contributes to metabolic syndrome, inflammatory bowel disease, macrophage activation syndromes, lung cancer susceptibility, autophagy disorders, or congenital disorders of fatty acid metabolism not yet mapped to Mendelian phenotypes. Future work should define ACOT11's acyl-CoA substrate specificity via kinetic assays with various chain lengths (C4-C20), determine crystal structure of full-length ACOT11 with bound myristic acid to identify START domain ligand pocket, investigate ACOT11 mutations in inflammatory disease patients with abnormal IL-1β production, assess ACOT11 role in autophagy flux using knockout models, develop ACOT11 inhibitors for treating fatty acid-driven cancers, explore ACOT11 biomarker potential in lung cancer screening, and test whether ACOT11 levels predict response to fatty acid metabolism inhibitors.
