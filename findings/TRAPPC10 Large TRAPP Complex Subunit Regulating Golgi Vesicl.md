## Description
TRAPPC10 (UniProt P48553) encodes a massive 1259 aa Trafficking Protein Particle Complex subunit 10, functioning as a critical scaffold for the TRAPP II and III complexes essential for ER-Golgi and intra-Golgi vesicle transport. InterPro analysis reveals a complex multi-domain architecture: an N-terminal domain (aa 16-328, IPR056913, PF23036) likely involved in complex nucleation; a central Immunoglobulin-like domain (aa 791-884, IPR056917, PF23604) suggesting protein-protein interaction capabilities distinct from typical antibody functions; and a large C-terminal domain (aa 1020-1246, IPR022233, PF12584) characteristic of the Trs130 family, crucial for TRAPP II specific activity. The entire protein belongs to the TRAPPC10/Trs130 family (IPR045126), directly linked to Golgi vesicle transport (GO:0048193). AlphaFold predicts a high-confidence structure (global pLDDT 79.0), indicating a well-folded, stable scaffold capable of bridging multiple trafficking factors. STRING interactions confirm TRAPPC10 as a central hub in the vesicle trafficking network with extremely high confidence partners: TRAPPC2 (0.999), TRAPPC5 (0.999), TRAPPC9 (0.999), TRAPPC2L (0.999), TRAPPC1 (0.998), TRAPPC4 (0.998), TRAPPC3 (0.997), TRAPPC8 (0.996), TRAPPC3L (0.989), and TRAPPC6A (0.937), defining its role as a core component of the multi-subunit TRAPP machinery. HPA expression shows ubiquitous detection across tissues with low specificity but enhanced levels in immune cells, consistent with a fundamental housekeeping role in secretory pathway maintenance. ClinVar reports 62 pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants, including copy number gains/losses on chromosome 21q and splice site mutations (c.1300+1G>A, c.286-3del), strongly associated with severe neurodevelopmental disorders including autism spectrum disorder, intellectual disability, epilepsy, dwarfism, and microcephaly (TRAPPopathy). Literature (16 papers) highlights: (1) Neurodevelopment—biallelic variants cause a recessive microcephalic TRAPPopathy disorder in humans and mice, disrupting neuronal migration and brain size; (2) Complex Assembly—TRAPPC10 acts as a large scaffold distinguishing TRAPP II (Golgi-to-plasma membrane) from TRAPP III (autophagy initiation) functions; (3) Disease Mechanism—loss of function leads to cargo accumulation, Golgi fragmentation, and impaired secretion of neurotrophic factors. Functional hypothesis: TRAPPC10 functions through: (1) Scaffold Assembly—bridging small GTPases (Rab1/Ypt1) and SNAREs via its extensive surface area to activate vesicle tethering; (2) Complex Specificity—the C-terminal domain confers TRAPP II specificity for post-Golgi transport, while N-terminal regions may recruit TRAPP III components for autophagy; (3) Neurodevelopmental Integrity—ensuring efficient trafficking of receptors and adhesion molecules critical for synapse formation and neuronal migration. Mechanistic proposal: In TRAPPopathy, truncating mutations or CNVs disrupt the scaffold integrity, preventing proper TRAPP complex assembly. This leads to a failure in vesicle tethering at the Golgi, causing accumulation of secretory cargo (e.g., Reelin, integrins) and subsequent defects in cortical layering and brain growth. The presence of an Ig-like domain suggests potential regulatory interactions with extracellular or luminal cues, adding a layer of control to vesicle fusion events. The combination of a large, well-folded structure, exclusive high-confidence interactions with other TRAPP subunits, ubiquitous expression, significant clinical variant burden (62 variants), and emerging literature on neurodevelopmental phenotypes positions TRAPPC10 as a pivotal but under-characterized regulator of cellular trafficking and brain development.
