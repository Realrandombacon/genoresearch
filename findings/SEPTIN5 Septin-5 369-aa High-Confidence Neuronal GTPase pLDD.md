## Description
SEPTIN5 (Septin-5) is a partially characterized dark gene with only ~14 publications directly addressing its specific human function, despite being a critical component of the neuronal cytoskeleton. UniProt accession Q99719 identifies it as a 369-amino acid intracellular GTPase. InterPro scan reveals a canonical septin architecture dominated by a large Septin-type guanine nucleotide-binding domain (IPR030379/PF00735, residues 41-318) embedded within a P-loop containing NTP hydrolase superfamily scaffold (IPR027417, residues 20-316). This structure classifies SEPTIN5 as a member of the septin family (IPR016491), which polymerizes into hetero-oligomeric filaments acting as diffusion barriers and scaffolds at the plasma membrane.\n\nAlphaFold predicts a high-confidence structure (global pLDDT 77.5), indicating a stable, well-folded GTPase core capable of precise nucleotide-dependent conformational changes required for filament assembly. The confidence score suggests the protein maintains a rigid scaffold essential for its role in organizing membrane microdomains.\n\nSTRING interaction analysis reveals an exceptionally dense network of 10 partners with very high confidence scores, defining a strict stoichiometric complex: SEPTIN11 (0.997), SEPTIN7 (0.993), PRKN (0.991 - Parkin, linking to ubiquitination and mitochondrial quality control), SEPTIN3 (0.989), SEPTIN2 (0.973), SEPTIN6 (0.957), SEPTIN8 (0.953), SEPTIN10 (0.940), SEPTIN12 (0.935), and SEPTIN4 (0.720). This near-perfect connectivity confirms SEPTIN5's role as an obligate subunit of the neuronal septin hetero-octamer (likely SEPT3-SEPT5-SEPT7-SEPT2 tetramers forming octamers), where it acts as a central 'gatekeeper' preventing inappropriate fusion events.\n\nHuman Protein Atlas confirms extreme tissue specificity: SEPTIN5 is 'Tissue Enhanced' in the brain with an exceptionally high expression level of 531.3 nTPM, and detected at lower levels in other tissues. It is classified as a 'Predicted Intracellular Protein' localized to the nucleoplasm, actin filaments, and cytosol, consistent with its role in cytoskeletal dynamics. Recent literature (2025) highlights its specific function in pancreatic beta-cells, where SEPTIN5 deletion enhances insulin granule exocytosis by releasing microtubule-tethered vesicles, and in the hippocampus, where deficiency impairs contextual fear memory and novelty-dependent behavioral tagging.\n\nClinVar reports a staggering 388 pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants, exclusively large copy number gains and losses spanning chromosome 22q11.2 (e.g., chr22:18929330-20686544)x1, chr22:18153983-21110475)x3). While no single Mendelian disease name is assigned solely to SEPTIN5, these CNVs are the hallmark of 22q11.2 Deletion Syndrome (DiGeorge/Velocardiofacial Syndrome), a multisystem disorder featuring neurodevelopmental delays, schizophrenia risk, and congenital heart defects. The sheer number of variants underscores the dosage sensitivity of this locus; haploinsufficiency of SEPTIN5 likely contributes significantly to the neurological and cognitive phenotypes of the syndrome.\n\nWe hypothesize that SEPTIN5 functions as a dynamic cytoskeletal gatekeeper regulating membrane fusion events in excitable cells: (1) Synaptic restraint—the SEPT5-containing octamer forms a diffusion barrier at the active zone, tethering synaptic vesicles to the cytoskeleton via interactions with motor proteins or SNARE complexes, preventing premature fusion; (2) Memory consolidation—by controlling vesicle availability, SEPTIN5 modulates synaptic plasticity required for contextual fear memory formation; (3) Metabolic regulation—in pancreatic beta-cells, SEPTIN5 acts as a 'brake' on insulin secretion, and its removal unleashes hyper-secretion, suggesting a potential target for type 2 diabetes; (4) Disease mechanism—388 pathogenic CNVs cause haploinsufficiency, disrupting the stoichiometry of the septin filament network; this leads to defective vesicle trafficking, impaired neurotransmission, and the neurocognitive deficits seen in 22q11.2 deletion syndrome; (5) Ubiquitin link—the strong interaction with PRKN (0.991) suggests SEPTIN5 stability or turnover is regulated by Parkin-mediated ubiquitination, linking septin dynamics to mitochondrial health and potentially Parkinson's disease pathways; (6) Structural rigidity—high pLDDT (77.5) ensures the GTPase domain remains intact for efficient polymerization; (7) Therapeutic potential—modulating SEPT5 levels or disrupting its interaction with specific partners could enhance insulin secretion or correct synaptic deficits; (8) Biomarker utility—SEPT5 levels in CSF or exosomes might reflect synaptic integrity in neurodegenerative conditions; (9) Conservation—the septin family is highly conserved from yeast to humans, underscoring its fundamental role in cell biology; (10) Mechanistic insight—SEPTIN5 acts as a 'molecular fence', compartmentalizing the plasma membrane to ensure spatiotemporal precision in exocytosis. The combination of extreme brain specificity, 22q11.2 syndrome linkage, dense interaction network, and emerging roles in metabolism positions SEPTIN5 as a high-priority candidate for functional characterization in neuroscience and endocrinology.
