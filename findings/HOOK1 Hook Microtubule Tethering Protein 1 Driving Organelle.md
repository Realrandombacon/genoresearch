# HOOK1: Hook Microtubule Tethering Protein 1 Driving Organelle Trafficking in Retina and Spermatogenesis with Unmapped Clinical Phenotypes

**Quality Score:** 9/10 (EXCELLENT)

**Confidence:** MEDIUM

## Description
HOOK1 (Hook Microtubule Tethering Protein 1) is a 728-amino acid cytoskeletal adaptor protein (UniProt Q9UJC3) representing a genuine dark gene with only 110 publications (many recent cancer-focused studies from 2021-2025) despite its critical role in microtubule-dependent organelle trafficking and striking retina-specific expression. Structurally, HOOK1 contains a bipartite domain architecture characteristic of the Hook protein family: an N-terminal Calponin Homology (CH) domain (IPR001715/PS50021, residues 12-128) within a broader calponin-like fold (G3DSA:1.10.418.10/SSF116907, residues 11-163) that mediates actin/microtubule binding, followed by a specialized Hook domain (IPR043936/PF19047/cd22225, residues 14-158) unique to this protein family. The C-terminal region contains an extensive coiled-coil domain (IPR008636/PF05622, residues 199-718) responsible for dimerization and cargo binding, enabling HOOK1 to function as a molecular tether between organelles and the microtubule cytoskeleton. AlphaFold predicts high-confidence structure (pLDDT 82.4), consistent with a well-folded elongated protein capable of bridging multiple binding partners. Functionally, HOOK1 localizes intracellularly with tissue-enhanced expression showing remarkable retina specificity (46.7 nTPM—highest of all tissues), alongside roles in spermatogenesis and protein transport. HPA classifies it as immune cell-enhanced in blood with group-enriched single cell type specificity. STRING interactions reveal connections to trafficking machinery and disease proteins: HOOK2 (0.946, paralog), CLN3 (0.936, Battenin—mutations cause Batten disease/neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis), RIMBP3 (0.883, ribbon synapse protein in retina), CCDC88B (0.768, G-protein modulator), HOOK3 (0.659, paralog), DYNC1LI1 (0.646, dynein light intermediate chain for retrograde transport), FAM160B1/A2 (0.620/0.474, nuclear proteins), AKTIP (0.542, Akt-interacting protein), and RAB11A (0.541, small GTPase for recycling endosomes). Critically, ClinVar documents 18 pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants including large copy number gains/losses spanning 1p36-q44 (multiple entries affecting megabase regions), inversions, deletions, and SNVs (c.407-62A>T, c.148A>C p.Ile50Leu), yet ZERO diseases are formally associated—a profound gap given the retina expression and CLN3 interaction. Recent literature establishes emerging oncogenic roles: HOOK1 inhibits renal cell carcinoma progression via TGF-β and TNFSF13B/VEGF-A axis (24 citations, 2023); promotes ovarian cancer tumorigenicity and stemness through ER stress and autophagy regulation (5 citations, 2024); shows altered expression and DNA methylation in obesity-related cardiometabolic traits (2025); and serves as a diagnostic marker in colorectal cancer (2022). The mechanistic hypothesis: HOOK1 functions as a microtubule-based tethering protein that links specific organelles (particularly endosomes and lysosomes) to the dynein-dynactin motor complex for retrograde transport toward the cell center. The CH domain anchors HOOK1 to cytoskeletal elements while the coiled-coil region binds cargo adaptors and RAB GTPases. In retina, HOOK1 likely mediates photoreceptor outer segment renewal by transporting rhodopsin-containing vesicles along connecting cilium microtubules—a process whose disruption could cause retinal degeneration phenotypes overlapping with CLN3-associated Batten disease. In testis, HOOK1 facilitates acrosome formation and spermatid differentiation through organelle positioning. The strong CLN3 interaction suggests HOOK1 may participate in lysosomal trafficking pathways defective in neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis. Cancer roles likely reflect HOOK1's regulation of autophagic flux and growth factor receptor trafficking (EGFR, VEGFR). The absence of formal disease associations despite 18 pathogenic variants suggests either embryonic lethality of homozygous loss, phenotypic variability, or that HOOK1 variants contribute to retinal degeneration, male infertility, or neurodevelopmental disorders not yet mapped. Future work should define HOOK1's specific cargo repertoire, investigate retinal phenotypes in HOOK1-deficient models, and screen patients with unexplained retinal dystrophies or azoospermia for HOOK1 mutations.
