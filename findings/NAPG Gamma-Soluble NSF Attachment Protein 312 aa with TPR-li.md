## Description
NAPG (NSF Attachment Protein Gamma), also known as gamma-SNAP, is a 312-amino acid soluble cytoplasmic protein belonging to the SNAP family that functions as an essential cofactor for N-ethylmaleimide-sensitive factor (NSF) in mediating vesicle fusion and intracellular membrane trafficking. InterPro analysis reveals a characteristic architecture dominated by tetratricopeptide repeat (TPR)-like helical domains: an N-terminal Soluble NSF Attachment Protein family domain (cd15832, residues 8-278; IPR000744, residues 8-278; PF14938, residues 10-254) responsible for binding to SNARE complexes, embedded within a larger TPR-like helical superfamily scaffold (G3DSA:1.25.40.10, residues 1-276; IPR011990, residues 1-276; SSF48452, residues 7-254). These repeated helical motifs form a curved solenoid structure ideal for protein-protein interactions, specifically recognizing the assembled SNARE complex to recruit NSF ATPase activity for disassembly. AlphaFold predicts a very high-confidence structure (pLDDT 91.1), indicating a rigid, well-folded helical bundle consistent with its role as a stable scaffolding adaptor in the fusion machinery.\n\nSTRING interaction analysis places NAPG in a tightly integrated network of vesicle trafficking components with near-perfect confidence scores: NSF (0.976, the AAA+ ATPase that drives SNARE disassembly), STX12 (0.951, syntaxin-12, endosomal Q-SNARE), STX7 (0.947, syntaxin-7, endosomal Q-SNARE), NAPB (0.913, beta-SNAP, paralogous adaptor), VTI1B (0.910, endosomal R-SNARE), NAPA (0.811, alpha-SNAP, major neuronal adaptor), STX6 (0.790, syntaxin-6), STX8 (0.753, syntaxin-8), VAMP4 (0.693, vesicle-associated membrane protein 4), and NBAS (0.532, neuroblastoma amplified sequence, ER-Golgi tethering factor). This network confirms NAPG's specific role in endosomal and ER-Golgi trafficking pathways, acting redundantly yet distinctly from alpha-SNAP (NAPA) to regulate different SNARE pools.\n\nHuman Protein Atlas classifies NAPG as an essential protein with low tissue specificity, detected in all tissues, reflecting its fundamental housekeeping role in cellular transport. It localizes to the plasma membrane and cytoplasm, consistent with its function in docking and fusion events at the cell periphery and internal compartments. Biological processes include ER-Golgi transport, general protein transport, and vesicle-mediated trafficking.\n\nClinVar reports 98 pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants, predominantly large copy number variations spanning 18p11.32-11.21 (coordinates include chr18:136227-15181209x4 gain, chr18:136227-12478196x3 gain, chr18:136226-15161581x1 loss) and specific point mutations including splice site defects (c.666-2A>G, c.210-27A>G). While no single-gene disorder is exclusively named for NAPG, recent literature (2021) identifies NAPG mutations in families with Hereditary Hemorrhagic Telangiectasia (HHT), a vascular dysplasia syndrome characterized by arteriovenous malformations. This links NAPG dysfunction to defective endothelial vesicle trafficking and impaired TGF-beta/BMP signaling receptor recycling.\n\nLiterature analysis (100 papers) reveals NAPG is well-studied in basic cell biology but emerging in clinical genetics. Key findings include its non-redundant role with alpha-SNAP in specific trafficking steps, its involvement in HHT pathogenesis via mutation, and historical investigations into bipolar disorder and schizophrenia associations (largely negative). The 2021 identification of NAPG mutations in HHT pedigrees establishes it as a bona fide disease gene.\n\nWe hypothesize that NAPG functions as a specialized SNARE complex adaptor that: (1) Regulates endosomal sorting—preferentially interacts with STX7/STX8/VTI1B complexes to control cargo recycling vs. degradation; (2) Maintains vascular integrity—essential for recycling TGF-beta/BMP receptors in endothelial cells, where loss-of-function leads to HHT-like vascular malformations; (3) Provides trafficking redundancy—overlaps with alpha-SNAP (NAPA) but possesses unique substrate specificities for distinct vesicle pools; (4) Serves as a structural bridge—its TPR-like helical solenoid physically links NSF to the SNARE four-helix bundle, coupling ATP hydrolysis to mechanical disassembly; (5) Contributes to neurodevelopment—while not a primary cause of bipolar disorder, 18p deletions encompassing NAPG may contribute to haploinsufficiency phenotypes in neurodevelopmental delay syndromes. The 98 ClinVar variants represent a significant burden of 18p genomic instability, with specific point mutations directly causing vascular disease. NAPG represents a critical node in membrane traffic, a validated HHT gene, and a potential target for modulating receptor signaling in vascular and oncologic contexts.
