# GUF1: Mitochondrial Elongation Factor G Homolog (EF4) Ensuring Translational Fidelity Under Stress and Causing West Syndrome via Ribosome Back-Translocation Mechanism

## Description
GUF1 (GTP-binding Elongation Factor GUF1, also known as mitochondrial EF4 or LepA homolog) is a partially characterized translational factor with 19 publications, emerging as a critical guardian of mitochondrial protein synthesis fidelity under stress conditions. This 669-amino acid mitochondrial GTPase (UniProt Q8N442) is the human homolog of bacterial LepA, an ancient elongation factor conserved from bacteria to humans. InterPro analysis reveals a canonical translation factor architecture: translational GTP-binding domain (IPR000795/G3DSA:3.40.50.300, positions 66-247), LepA/EF4 N-terminal domain (cd01890, positions 69-246), EF4 Domain II (cd03699/G3DSA:2.40.30.10, positions 254-339), EF4 Domain III (cd16260/G3DSA:3.30.70.870, positions 355-430), EFG Domain V-like (IPR000640/G3DSA:3.30.70.240, positions 469-556), and C-terminal lepA_C domain (cd03709/G3DSA:3.30.70.2570, positions 471-624). AlphaFold predicts high-confidence structure (pLDDT 81.6), indicating a well-folded multi-domain GTPase capable of large conformational changes required for ribosome interaction.\n\nSTRING interactions confirm GUF1 operates within the core translation machinery, linking mitochondrial and cytosolic ribosomes: POLR3A (0.937, RNA polymerase III subunit), MRPL20 (0.904, mitochondrial ribosomal protein L20), MRPS6 (0.904, mitochondrial ribosomal protein S6), RPL5 (0.901, cytosolic ribosomal protein L5), RPS3 (0.900, cytosolic ribosomal protein S3), RPL11 (0.900), RPL23 (0.894), RPS11 (0.893), MRPS9 (0.664), and MRPL32 (0.593). The high-confidence links to both mitochondrial (MRPL/MRPS) and cytosolic (RPL/RPS) ribosomal proteins suggest GUF1 may interact with both organellar and cytoplasmic translation systems, though its primary role is mitochondrial.\n\nHPA shows low tissue specificity with expression detected in many tissues, consistent with essential housekeeping function in energy production. Subcellular localization includes mitochondria and nucleoplasm. HPA explicitly lists disease involvement: Epilepsy. Biological processes involve protein biosynthesis with hydrolase molecular function (GTP hydrolysis). ClinVar contains 41 pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants, including missense mutations affecting critical functional residues (p.Val662Ile, p.Val652Ala, p.Asp527Glu, p.Thr310Ala, p.Gly403Ala) and splice site variants (c.1716-95G>T, c.426+4A>T), as well as large copy number gains on chromosome 4p. The 2015 landmark study identified homozygous GUF1 variants as the cause of West syndrome (infantile epileptic spasms syndrome), establishing GUF1 as a neurodevelopmental disease gene. Conservation from bacteria (LepA) to yeast, mouse, and human indicates extreme evolutionary constraint.\n\nHYPOTHESIS: GUF1 functions as a 'ribosome rescue' factor that ensures translational fidelity in mitochondria under suboptimal conditions (e.g., oxidative stress, limited tRNA availability, damaged mRNA). Mechanistically, GUF1 binds to post-translocational ribosomes and catalyzes back-translocation of misaligned tRNAs from the P/E and A/P sites to the P/P and A/A sites, allowing a second chance for correct codon-anticodon pairing before peptide bond formation. This proofreading activity prevents accumulation of misfolded mitochondrial proteins that could disrupt OXPHOS complex assembly. Under normal conditions, GUF1 is dispensable, but under stress (heat shock, oxidative damage), it becomes essential for maintaining respiratory chain function. In pancreatic β-cells, GUF1 overexpression improves insulin secretion and cell survival in type 2 diabetes models, suggesting therapeutic potential. In myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury, tanshinone IIA derivatives exert cardioprotection via GUF1-dependent pathways, likely by preserving mitochondrial translation during hypoxic stress. Pathogenic missense variants likely impair GTPase activity or ribosome binding, leading to defective mitochondrial proteostasis, bioenergetic failure in neurons, and the hyperexcitability underlying infantile spasms. The nucleoplasmic localization hints at potential non-canonical roles in nuclear transcription or cytosolic translation regulation.
