## Description
ATP5MC3 (ATP synthase membrane subunit c locus 3, also known as ATP5G3, P3, DYTSPG) is a 142 amino acid mitochondrial inner membrane protein (UniProt P48201) with high-confidence well-folded architecture (AlphaFold pLDDT 71.6), characteristic of F0 complex subunit c proteolipids. The protein contains a conserved ATP synthase F0 complex subunit C domain (IPR000454/PF00137/cd18182, residues 1-142/76-138/74-138) that forms the proton-conducting c-ring of the F0 motor. A V-ATPase proteolipid subunit C-like domain (IPR002379, residues 76-138) and a critical DCCD-binding site (IPR020537, residues 104-125) containing the essential glutamate residue for proton translocation are present. The protein belongs to the F/V-ATP synthase subunit C superfamily (IPR035921/IPR038662/G3DSA:1.20.20.10, residues 69-141), with molecular functions in proton transmembrane transporter activity (GO:0015078) and proton motive force-driven ATP synthesis (GO:0015986), placing ATP5MC3 within oxidative phosphorylation and the proton-transporting ATP synthase complex (GO:0045259).\n\nSTRING interaction analysis reveals an exceptionally tight network of ATP synthase subunits with near-perfect confidence scores: ATP5MC1 (0.999, ATP synthase membrane subunit c locus 1), ATP5F1C (0.999, ATP synthase subunit gamma), ATP5PB (0.999, ATP synthase subunit beta), ATP5F1D (0.998, ATP synthase subunit delta), ATP5MC2 (0.998, ATP synthase membrane subunit c locus 2), ATP5PD (0.998, ATP synthase subunit d), MT-ATP6 (0.998, ATP synthase membrane subunit 6, mitochondrially encoded), ATP5F1A (0.998, ATP synthase subunit alpha), ATP5PF (0.997, ATP synthase subunit f), and ATP5F1B (0.990, ATP synthase subunit beta). These interactions confirm ATP5MC3 is a stoichiometric component of the F1F0-ATP synthase holoenzyme, forming the c-ring rotor that couples proton flow to rotational catalysis.\n\nHPA expression reveals tissue enhancement in heart muscle (1149.4 nTPM) and tongue (1014.6 nTPM), detected in all tissues, consistent with the universal requirement for ATP production. The protein is classified as a disease-related gene, metabolic protein, potential drug target, and predicted membrane protein/transporter. Critically, disease involvement includes dystonia, hereditary spastic paraplegia, and neurodegeneration, indicating ATP5MC3 mutations cause movement disorders and neuronal degeneration.\n\nClinVar documents 39 pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants, including missense mutations affecting critical residues (c.185C>T, p.Thr62Ile; c.265G>C, p.Gly89Arg; c.236G>T, p.Gly79Val; c.319C>G, p.Pro107Ala), intronic variants (c.120+20A>T), 5' UTR variants (c.-72G>C), and large copy number losses (2q31.1-32.2, 2q31.1-31.3 deletions) and gains (2p25.3-q37.3 amplifications). The missense mutations likely disrupt c-ring assembly, proton binding, or rotor-stator interactions. The DCCD-binding site glutamate (position ~104-125) is essential for proton translocation; nearby mutations may impair proton conductance.\n\nConservation is strong across vertebrates: human (P48201, 142 aa), mouse (P56384, 141 aa), rat (Q71S46, 142 aa), cow (Q3ZC75, 141 aa), indicating an ancient and indispensable function in bioenergetics.\n\nMechanistic hypothesis: ATP5MC3 functions as a core component of the F0 c-ring rotor, forming a hairpin structure with two transmembrane helices connected by a cytoplasmic loop. The essential glutamate residue in the DCCD-binding site undergoes protonation/deprotonation during rotation, driving the c-ring to rotate relative to the a-subunit stator. This rotation is transmitted via the gamma-epsilon stalk to the F1 catalytic core, driving conformational changes that synthesize ATP from ADP and Pi. Three paralogous c-subunit genes exist in humans (ATP5MC1, ATP5MC2, ATP5MC3), potentially allowing tissue-specific expression or functional specialization. The heart muscle enrichment suggests ATP5MC3 may be the predominant isoform in cardiac tissue, where high ATP demand requires efficient oxidative phosphorylation. Loss of ATP5MC3 causes defective c-ring assembly, impaired proton translocation, reduced ATP synthesis, and increased ROS production. Neurons and muscle are particularly vulnerable to bioenergetic failure, explaining the dystonia, hereditary spastic paraplegia, and neurodegeneration phenotypes. ATP5MC3 represents a validated cause of movement disorders and a critical node in mitochondrial energy metabolism.
