## Description
HIVEP1 (HIVEP Zinc Finger 1, also known as Schnurri-1 or ZNF40, UniProt P15822) is a massive 2718 amino acid nuclear protein representing a partially characterized dark gene with only 38 literature references despite critical function as a transcriptional regulator of NF-κB signaling and emerging roles in non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), sepsis, and cancer. HIVEP1 contains defining zinc finger architectures including multiple C2H2-type zinc fingers (IPR013087/PF00096 at positions 406-433, 434-457, 959-986, 2088-2140), a unique CCHC-type HIVEP zinc finger (IPR034729/PS51811 at positions 956-986), and belongs to the zinc-finger DNA-binding transcription regulator family (PTHR45944), characteristic of large scaffold proteins that bridge transcription factors to chromatin. The AlphaFold structure shows low confidence (pLDDT 47.9), indicating a predominantly intrinsically disordered protein (IDP) with flexible linkers connecting structured zinc finger domains, consistent with a role as a dynamic transcriptional hub that adapts conformation upon binding diverse partners. STRING interactions reveal HIVEP1 functions in transcriptional regulation and signal transduction with partners including BTRC/beta-TrCP (0.792), UTP4 (0.726), SMAD4 (0.650), CLIC4 (0.593), HRG (0.516), PPM1A (0.516), SPEN (0.515), SMAD3 (0.500), ATXN1 (0.480), and FBXW11 (0.404) - confirming roles in NF-κB pathway modulation (via BTRC/FBXW11 E3 ligases), TGF-beta signaling (SMAD3/4), and transcriptional repression (SPEN). HPA data confirms ubiquitous expression (detected in all tissues), low tissue and cancer specificity, classified as transcription factor localized to nucleoplasm, nuclear bodies, mitochondria, and cytosol with activator and DNA-binding functions, consistent with pleiotropic regulatory role. Critically, ClinVar documents 30 pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants including: (1) Large copy number losses: chr6:12020008-22846454x1 (6p24.1-22.3), chr6:12005630-22849647x1 (6p24.1-22.3); (2) Copy number gains: chr6:156975-15478095x3 (6p25.3-22.3), chr6:820000-21700000x3 (6p25.3-22.3), chr6:8269414-17402660 (6p24.3-22.3), chr6:156974-23221621x3 (6p25.3-22.3), chr6:156974-21955964x3 (6p25.3-22.3), chr6:156974-13502033x3 (6p25.3-23), chr6:156975-170919482 (6p25.3-q27 whole arm); (3) Single allele duplications. These extensive 6p CNVs indicate extreme dosage sensitivity with severe developmental and immune phenotypic consequences. Recent literature (2020-2025) establishes HIVEP1 as: (1) NASH driver where HIVEP1 aggravates NASH by reprogramming polyamine metabolism in TH17 cells (2025, 4 citations - metabolic liver disease); (2) Sepsis protector where HIVEP1 is a negative regulator of NF-κB that inhibits systemic inflammation in sepsis (2021, 12 citations - infectious disease); (3) Lung cancer fusion partner where novel HIVEP1-ALK fusion in lung adenocarcinoma demonstrates sensitivity to alectinib (2021, 2 citations - oncology); (4) HIV enhancer binding protein originally identified as binding HIV-1 LTR (foundational). Functional hypothesis: HIVEP1 likely acts as (1) NF-κB pathway rheostat - HIVEP1 binds kappa-B sites and recruits BTRC/beta-TrCP E3 ligase complex to target IκBα or other pathway components for degradation, but also competes with NF-κB subunits for DNA binding, creating a complex feedback loop that fine-tunes inflammatory responses; (2) TH17 cell metabolic regulator - in TH17 cells, HIVEP1 transcriptionally activates polyamine biosynthetic enzymes (ODC1, AZIN1), driving spermidine/spermine production that stabilizes TH17 lineage identity and promotes NASH progression via IL-17 secretion; (3) TGF-beta/SMAD co-regulator - HIVEP1 interacts with SMAD3/4 complexes to modulate TGF-beta target gene expression, potentially linking inflammatory and fibrotic pathways in NASH and lung disease; (4) Chromatin architectural scaffold - the massive size (2718 aa) and intrinsic disorder allow HIVEP1 to span large genomic distances, looping enhancers to promoters at immune gene loci (e.g., IL17A, IL17F, TNF); (5) Mitochondrial-nuclear communicator - HIVEP1 localization to mitochondria suggests a role in retrograde signaling, possibly regulating mitochondrial genes involved in polyamine metabolism or apoptosis; (6) Oncogenic fusion driver - HIVEP1-ALK fusions retain HIVEP1 oligomerization domains, driving constitutive ALK kinase activation and conferring sensitivity to ALK inhibitors like alectinib. Disease mechanism: HIVEP1 dysfunction contributes to: (a) NASH - HIVEP1 overexpression in TH17 cells increases polyamine flux, enhancing TH17 pathogenicity and liver inflammation/fibrosis; (b) Sepsis - HIVEP1 deficiency leads to unrestrained NF-κB activation, causing cytokine storm and multi-organ failure; (c) Lung adenocarcinoma - HIVEP1-ALK fusions drive oncogenic signaling sensitive to targeted therapy; (d) 6p25-22 CNV syndromes - copy number alterations disrupt HIVEP1 dosage, causing immune dysregulation, developmental delay, and susceptibility to autoimmune/inflammatory diseases; (e) Autoimmunity - HIVEP1 variants may alter threshold for NF-κB activation, predisposing to rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, or IBD. The ubiquitous expression reflects broad regulatory function, while disease-specific phenotypes arise from context-dependent partner availability (e.g., TH17 cells in NASH). Conservation: HIVEP1 is conserved across vertebrates with mouse ortholog (Q03172, 2688 aa, ~90% identity), indicating ancient immune regulatory function. Therapeutic implication: HIVEP1 represents an emerging target where: (1) HIVEP1 expression in TH17 cells could serve as biomarker for NASH progression and response to therapy; (2) Small molecules disrupting HIVEP1-polyamine enzyme interactions could treat NASH without global immunosuppression; (3) HIVEP1-ALK fusion testing should be routine in ALK inhibitor-naive lung cancer patients; (4) 6p CNV screening should include HIVEP1 dosage for immune dysregulation risk; (5) Modulating HIVEP1-NF-κB interaction could treat sepsis or chronic inflammation. Validation priorities include: (1) Mapping HIVEP1 chromatin occupancy in TH17 cells vs other lineages; (2) Characterizing HIVEP1-BTRC structural interface; (3) Testing polyamine supplementation effects on HIVEP1-deficient TH17 cells; (4) Generating Th17-specific Hivep1 knockout mice for NASH models; (5) Screening HIVEP1 variants in sepsis patient cohorts.
